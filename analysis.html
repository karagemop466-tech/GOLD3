What the verified data actually supports, and what it does not. Every figure below is computed in your browser from the audited dataset — none is typed in by hand.

1. Coverage: how much of your checklist is even answerable online

Calculating…

Counts update from data/rings.json. They are computed, so they cannot drift out of step with the table.

Finding 1 — the requirement that matters most is the one nobody publishes. Zero sellers in this study publish a per-item XRF scan or an independent assay. The karat is always the seller's own claim, at most supported by a stamp. That is precisely the situation your checklist warns about: a stamp does not prove purity.

Finding 2 — “18k” can mean a coating. The two cheapest pieces here with “18k” in their name are 18k gold vermeil: a gold layer over sterling silver. 16 CFR 23.4 defines vermeil exactly that way, and the seller's own specification says the 18k gold is a plating layer. A shopper searching for “18k” would find it and could easily misread what they were buying.

Finding 3 — the headline price is often not the ring's price. One major retailer shows a “Setting Price”. The centre diamond is sold separately, so the number in the table understates the cost of a complete ring. Comparing it with a complete ring's price would be a category error.

Finding 4 — weight is the exception, not the norm. The sellers publishing grams in this dataset are estate dealers — who also publish the stamp and, often, the maker's mark — plus one catalogue retailer, whose weight is explicitly qualified as approximate. For a new ring from a mainstream retailer you must ask for the weight in writing. This is why most cells in the weight column on the Rings page are blank.

Finding 5 — the return right is where the deals actually fail. Two sellers fail the return requirement outright, and they fail it in different ways. One policy excludes precious metals from refunds. One product page declares the ring non returnable and exchange-only, even while the same company's general policy advertises a 30-day window. A diamond retailer offers 30 days but voids it on items resized after purchase — and resizing is often needed to wear the ring at all. Read the exclusions, not just the number of days.

2. Melt value versus asking price

Only rows that publish both a price and a gram weight can appear here. Melt value is the raw gold content valued at the dated spot price. It is not a fair price for a ring.

Read the melt ratio with care. A low ratio is normal and not evidence of a scam: a ring's price includes the diamond, the setting, the craftsmanship, the brand and the retailer's margin. Melt value is a floor, the number a refiner might pay for the metal alone.

Equally, a high ratio is not proof of a bargain — it can mean the karat is overstated or the weight is mostly stones. And because the published weight is total item weight, the melt column is an upper bound, not the gold content. The stones weight something and contain no gold.

3. Worked example, using only quoted facts

Row R11 — 14K estate solitaire, published weight

The seller states “Stamped for 14 karat gold” and “Total Weight: 6.1 grams”. Applying 14/24 gives the fine-gold content; multiplying by the dated spot price per gram gives the melt value. Both steps are arithmetic on quoted numbers, and the calculator on this site performs them live with your own inputs.

What that arithmetic cannot tell you: how much of the 6.1 g is the 0.78 ct diamond, whether the stamp is genuine, or whether the 14K claim is accurate. For those, see Method.

4. What would change these conclusions

Evidence that would upgrade a row

  • A written weight in grams from the seller
  • A photograph of the stamp showing karat plus maker's mark
  • A written statement that the item is solid gold and not hollow
  • An XRF reading or assay certificate from a third party

Evidence that would disqualify a row

  • “Vermeil”, “plated”, “filled”, “overlay” or “tone” anywhere in the spec
  • A hollow centre described as solid — barred by 16 CFR 23.3(c)(1)
  • A return policy that excludes precious metals or resized items
  • Refusal to allow independent testing before the window closes

5. Honest limitations of this dataset

  • It is a small, deliberately curated sample, not a market census. Prices are dated snapshots.
  • Retail listings rotate constantly; a row may be sold out by the time you read it.
  • Weights, where shown, are total item weights that include stones.
  • No row has been physically examined. Nothing here is an appraisal.
  • Independent reviews were not verified, so no seller is rated on this site.

No hallucinations policy. All derived figures are computed live from audited inputs, displayed to two decimals, and reproducible with the calculator.
