No hallucinations — read this first

This project does not invent prices, weights, purities, or reviews. A figure appears here only if it can be traced to a page you can open yourself. Anything unconfirmed is shown as unverified and listed on the Flagged for Review page. When in doubt, the value is blank — not estimated.

  • Prices are copied from the seller's official page, dated, and linked. No price is averaged, projected, or carried over from an earlier visit.
  • Weights are copied from the seller's own written statement in grams. No weight is inferred from karat, ring size, photo, or "typical" practice.
  • Purity is only ever the seller's claim, quoted verbatim. No seller in this study publishes an independent assay, so every row still needs XRF testing after delivery.
  • Reputation is not scored. No star rating is asserted, because no rating was verified against a primary review source.
  • Derived numbers (per-gram gold value, melt value, price per pure gram) are computed in your browser from the audited spot price and the quoted weight — never stored as a rounded claim.

An automated audit re-checks these rules on every change: python3 scripts/audit.py. It fails the build if a price lacks its quoted source text, if a gram figure is not present in its own quote, or if a "pass" verdict was reached without the seller actually saying “solid”.

The LBMA Gold Price is the global benchmark, but its historical tables are licence-gated, so this site uses the World Gold Council's published spot price instead and says so. Spot is a wholesale number; a retail ring costs more than its gold.

What this project is, and is not

It is a verification workbench: a small set of real, currently listed gold rings, each row tied to its seller's page, with the gaps made visible.

It is not a price-comparison engine across thousands of SKUs, and it is not an appraisal. It cannot tell you a specific ring is genuinely 14K — only a jeweller with an XRF gun or an assay can, after delivery.

Start with Rings, then read Flagged for Review before you consider buying anything.

The checklist, and how far each item can be proven online

  • ①Confirm the karat Printed on the seller's page for every row. Higher karat means more gold: 24K is pure, 18K is 75%, 14K is 58.3%, 10K is 41.7%. Fineness table →
  • ②Confirm “solid gold”, not plating The single most important word to look for. Two rows here are 18k vermeil — a gold layer over sterling silver — and would fool a shopper scanning for “18k”. See flag S1 →
  • ③Hallmark plus maker's mark Federal rules require a quality mark on gold to be accompanied by the maker's name or trademark. Almost no online listing shows either. Legal basis →
  • ④Weight in grams Estate jewellers and one catalogue retailer publish it in this study; most others do not. Where it is absent it stays blank and flagged, because a weight you cannot source is a number you invented.
  • ⑤Seller reputation and real contact details Phone and email are recorded only where the seller publishes them. Independent review scores are deliberately left empty rather than fabricated.
  • ⑥Price versus gold value Computed live in the calculator and in Analysis — but only for rows that publish a weight.
  • ⑦A return path that survives inspection Return windows, exclusions on resized and engraved items, and a policy that declares precious metals non-refundable. Compare sellers →
  • ⑧Pay by credit card Noted where a seller hints at irreversible methods such as bank wire. See flag RET3 →
  • ⑨Keep the documentation Listings, receipts and certificates. Some return paths depend on you still holding the grading report. See flag RET5 →
  • ⑩Independent verification on arrival XRF or a professional assay, before wearing or resizing. This is the step no website can do for you, and the only step that actually proves purity.

Three findings worth your attention before you shop.

Returns can be designed out. A large warehouse retailer's own policy states that precious metals are non-refundable, and a luxury brand's own product page states that a flagship engagement ring is non returnable and may only be exchanged. Either one would defeat your “return it after an independent test” rule.

“18k” can mean a coating. Entry-level listings in this dataset are 18k gold vermeil, where the “18k” describes a plating layer over sterling silver rather than the body of the ring.

All three are quoted with links on the Flagged for Review page.
