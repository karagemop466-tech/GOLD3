The rules

What the words are legally allowed to mean

This page exists so that no claim on the site is my opinion. These are the actual U.S. rules on gold content, plating, “solid”, hallmarks and tolerances, quoted from the eCFR and the U.S. Code, with the date each one was read.

Why this matters for the checklist: 15 U.S.C. § 297(b) is the legal basis for “karat stamp plus a maker's mark”. It is not folklore — when a quality mark is stamped, the maker's trademark or name must be applied too, in lettering at least as large and as close as possible to it.

Saying "gold" requires the karat right next to it

A product may only be called "Gold" if the correct karat fineness immediately precedes the word Gold, in equally conspicuous type. A bare "gold" claim is a violation.

“Use of the word "Gold" or any abbreviation to describe all or part of an industry product (including the surface layer of a coated product) composed throughout of an alloy of gold (i.e., gold that is less than 24 karats), unless a correct designation of the karat fineness of the alloy immediately precedes the word "Gold" or its abbreviation, and such fineness designation is of at least equal conspicuousness.”

16 CFR § 23.3 — Misrepresentation as to gold content (FTC Jewelry Guides) ↗ · read 2026-10-22

A karat stamp must be accompanied by a maker's / dealer's mark

Under the National Gold and Silver Stamping Act, when a quality mark indicating gold is stamped on an article, the manufacturer or dealer must also apply its trademark or name — by the same means, in lettering at least as large, and as close as possible to the quality mark. This is the legal basis for the checklist rule: karat stamp PLUS maker's mark.

“shall— (A) Apply or cause to be applied to that article a trademark of such persons, which has been duly registered or applied for registration under the laws of the United States ... or the name of such person”
“Each identifying trademark or name applied to any article of merchandise in compliance with clause (A) of this subsection shall be applied to that article by the same means as that used in applying the quality mark or stamp appearing thereon, in type or lettering at least as large as that used in such quality mark or stamp, and in a position as close as possible to that quality mark or stamp.”

15 U.S.C. § 297(b) — Stamping plated articles (identifying trademark) ↗ · read 2026-10-22

How much a stamp is legally allowed to be wrong

The actual fineness may not be more than 3/1000 below the stamped fineness on a solder-free sample, and not more than 7/1000 below when the whole article (including solder) is assayed as one piece.

“the actual fineness of such gold or alloy shall not be less by more than three one-thousandth parts than the fineness indicated by the mark stamped, branded, engraved, or printed upon any part of such article”
“shall not be less by more than three one-thousandth parts, in the case of a watchcase or flatware, or than seven one-thousandth parts, in the case of any other such article, than the fineness indicated by the mark”

15 U.S.C. § 295 — Standard of fineness of gold articles; deviation ↗ · read 2026-10-22

Falsely marking gold is unlawful, not just bad service

It is unlawful to stamp or label an article of gold jewelry with a mark indicating a greater fineness than the article actually has.

“any mark or word indicating or designed or intended to indicate that the gold or silver or alloy of either of said metals in such article is of a greater degree of fineness than the actual fineness or quality of such gold, silver, or alloy”

15 U.S.C. § 294 — Importation or transportation of falsely marked gold or silver ware prohibited ↗ · read 2026-10-22

"Gold plate", "gold filled" and "gold overlay" have defined meanings — none of them is solid gold

Gold plate / gold overlay / rolled gold plate = a gold-alloy plating of at least 1/40th of the article's metal weight. "Gold filled" may only be used at 1/20th or more. Electroplate requires at least 0.175 microns of fine gold. None of these are a solid gold ring.

“When such plating constitutes at least 1/40th of the weight of the metal in the entire article ... When such plating constitutes at least 1/20th of the weight of the metal in the entire article, the term "Gold Filled" may be used.”
“has a minimum thickness throughout equivalent to 0.175 microns (approximately 7/1,000,000ths of an inch) of fine gold”

16 CFR § 23.3(c)(2)–(3) (FTC Jewelry Guides) ↗ · read 2026-10-22

"Vermeil" is gold over sterling silver, never solid gold

Vermeil = sterling silver base coated with gold (or ≥10K gold alloy) at least 2.5 microns thick. It is a silver ring with gold on the outside.

“may be described or marked as "vermeil" if it consists of a base of sterling silver coated or plated on all significant surfaces with gold, or gold alloy of not less than 10 karat fineness ... and a minimum thickness throughout equivalent to two and one half (21⁄2) microns”

16 CFR § 23.4 — Misuse of the word "vermeil" ↗ · read 2026-10-22

"Solid" may not be used for a hollow ring

A ring with a hollow centre or interior should not be marked or described as "solid" or "14 Kt. Solid Gold" — the hollow must be disclosed next to the word Gold.

“Such products should not be marked or described as "solid" as being solidly of gold or of a gold alloy.”
“when the composition of such a product is 14 karat gold alloy, it should not be described or marked as either "14 Kt. Solid Gold" or as "Solid 14 Kt. Gold."”

16 CFR § 23.3(c)(1) (FTC Jewelry Guides) ↗ · read 2026-10-22

White gold is usually rhodium-plated — that must be disclosed

It is deceptive to fail to disclose a rhodium surface layer on products described as precious metal. So "14K white gold" plus rhodium plating is normal, but the plating has to be stated.

“It is unfair or deceptive to fail to disclose a surface-layer application of rhodium on products marked or described as precious metal.”

16 CFR § 23.7 — Disclosure of surface-layer application of rhodium ↗ · read 2026-10-22

Two-metal rings (e.g. 18k band + platinum head) must list metals by weight

Where a product contains more than one precious metal, the relative quantity of each must not be misrepresented and metals should be listed from greatest to least by weight.

“Marketers should list precious metals in the order of their relative weight in the product from greatest to least (i.e., leading with the predominant metal).”

16 CFR § 23.8 — Misrepresentation as to products containing more than one precious metal ↗ · read 2026-10-22

The guides bind sellers at every level of trade

The FTC guides apply to manufacturers, suppliers and retailers, and to all marketing representations, direct or implied.

“These guides apply to persons, partnerships, or corporations, at every level of the trade (including but not limited to manufacturers, suppliers, and retailers) engaged in the business of offering for sale, selling, or distributing industry products.”

16 CFR § 23.0 — Scope and application ↗ · read 2026-10-22

Independent verification: what to ask for

XRF (X-ray fluorescence) is the usual non-destructive test: it reads the elemental composition of the surface without cutting the ring. Ask the jeweller to test the band in more than one place, and to weigh the ring on a calibrated scale to 0.01 g, because plating and solder can make a single surface reading optimistic.

Honesty note (flagged): the two legal tolerances above are quoted from statute. The practice of XRF testing is described here as general trade practice — this project has not found a single official U.S. standard that prescribes XRF as the required test, so treat the method description as guidance, not as a regulation.
