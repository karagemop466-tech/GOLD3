Standards the checklist rests on

Your requirements are not folklore — most are codified in US federal rules. This page quotes the governing text so the checklist can be defended, and so a seller's wording can be read against it.

Primary source. 16 CFR Part 23 — Guides for the Jewelry, Precious Metals, and Pewter Industries, published by the Federal Trade Commission, in the electronic Code of Federal Regulations. The maker's-mark rule traces to the National Stamping Act, 15 U.S.C. 294–297.

Reading a seller's wording against the rules

What the listing saysWhat it may actually meanRule
“18k Gold Vermeil” Sterling silver body with a gold coating at least 2.5 microns thick. Not solid gold. 16 CFR 23.4(b)
“14k Yellow Gold” (karat only) A gold alloy of that fineness. Does not by itself rule out a hollow interior. 16 CFR 23.3(c)(1)
“14K Solid Gold” on a hollow ring Improper. Hollow items should not be described as solid. 16 CFR 23.3(c)(1)
“White gold” Gold alloy, usually rhodium plated on the surface; the plating wears. 16 CFR 23.7
“Gold plated” / “gold filled” A coating or mechanically bonded layer, with minimum-thickness conditions. 16 CFR 23.3(b), (c)
A karat stamp alone Incomplete unless the maker's trademark or name is also applied. 15 U.S.C. 297 · 16 CFR 23.10
“Total weight” Weight of the whole item, stones included — not gold weight. 16 CFR 23.18

Assay tolerance

When an independent jeweller tests your ring, the result is judged against a tolerance, not a perfect number: three parts per thousand for gold parts without solder, seven parts per thousand where solder is present. Cited at 16 CFR 23.3, footnote 32, referencing 15 U.S.C. 295.

What these rules do not do. They govern how sellers and makers may describe an item. They do not certify any individual ring. No government body issues a purity certificate for a retail ring — only an assay or XRF test on the actual object does that.
